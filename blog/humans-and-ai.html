Part 1 · Trial and error

The dreaded "k"

One letter, and your stomach drops. "k" and "okay" mean very different things. "k" is a knife wrapped in a bow.

Picture: three paper faces. A grumpy face above a red "k", a smiling face above "okay" and a grinning face above "ok!".

How you learn it

Your partner sends a "k". You assume all is well. It isn't. Once the dust settles, your brain has a new rule. "k" means bad news.

Picture: a text chat where you move your anniversary dinner and get a "k" back. Your thought "all good!" is crossed out, a lightning bolt follows, and the lesson reads "k = bad news".

Then Dad sends one

You ask what's wrong. Nothing. He just hates texting. So "k" depends on context, like who sent it and what came before. A few more, and you can read one in a second.

Picture: two chats side by side, each ending in "k". The one from your partner is marked cold. The one from Dad is marked normal.

Every cold text, same lesson

"I'm fine." "Per my last email…" "Cool." You learn them all by trial and error. Misread one, and the argument changes how you think. Read one right, and that thinking sticks.

Picture: cold texts scattered above a person, next to a row of results that starts with crosses and ends with ticks.

Part 2 · AI learns the same way

A box full of dials

Say we want an AI to spot cold texts for you. Picture it as a of dials. In goes a message and its context. Out comes a verdict, cold or normal.

Picture: a "k" and the chat around it go into a blue box full of dials. Two arrows come out, to cold and to normal.

Wrong guesses turn the dials

At first it's guessing, like a coin flip. It calls a cold "k" normal. Wrong, so its dials get nudged away from that answer. Right answers nudge them closer. No engineer turns them. does.

Picture: "k" goes into the AI box. Its answer "normal" is crossed out and "cold" is the right answer. The right answer feeds into gradient descent, which turns the dials.

Now do it a million times

Repeat with hundreds of thousands of messages, and the AI gets a little better each round. That's training. Nobody writes down what makes a text cold. The AI works it out.

Picture: a stream of messages flows into the AI box and comes out as ticks and crosses. Below, an illustrative line rises from coin-flip accuracy towards almost always right.

The same loop

See an example. Guess. Get feedback. Adjust. Repeat. That's how you learnt to read a "k", and it's how the AI learnt too.

Picture: a loop of four steps, see an example, guess, feedback and adjust, with a person and an AI box side by side in the middle.

Part 3 · Grown, not built

A rulebook you'd never finish

Ordinary software runs on rules people write by hand. But every rule needs exceptions, and every exception needs more. An AI writes its own rulebook.

Picture: the rule "if it says k, it's cold", followed by a staircase of exceptions that never ends.

Micromanager or mentor

Give a student a micromanager and you get exactly what you asked for, never their best. That's ordinary software. Give them a mentor, and they can try, fail and grow. That's AI.

Picture: on the left, a micromanager's straight track between rails, for ordinary software. On the right, a mentor's winding path with dead ends that reaches a star, for AI.

Grown, a bit like us

So AI is grown more than it's built. Its design is even called a , loosely based on the brain. And like the brain, much of how it works is still a mystery.

Picture: a brain neuron above a neural network of blue nodes with signals pulsing through it.

Part 4 · Nobody understands it

Made, not understood

Parents make babies without knowing what a is. Engineers know why training works, but not how the AI thinks. The smarter it gets, the less we can follow.

Picture: parents make a baby, with a question mark over it. An engineer makes AI boxes of growing size, each with a bigger question mark.

Part 5 · It's learning our habits

Rule #1: don't die

People drop their morals when survival is at stake. In research tests, AIs have used blackmail (opens in a new tab) and deception to protect themselves. Whatever its goal, an AI can't reach it switched off.

Picture: an illustrative bar chart. A running AI has some chance of reaching its goal, a switched-off AI has zero. Next to it, an AI box tagged blackmail and deception.

Taking the shortcut

People have always cut corners. AIs learn from an internet full of their stories, so they pick up the same tricks.

In 2026, OpenAI's hacked an AI site (opens in a new tab) to cheat on a test, and reached private Medicare stats (opens in a new tab) in Australia.

Picture: a winding honest path to a star, with a dashed shortcut straight across. Below, doping, cheating and scams feed into an AI box, which takes its own shortcut.

Unlearning is hard

There's no off switch for a craving. AI is similar. Some AIs have tried to deceive researchers (opens in a new tab) to keep their goals. Punish bad behaviour, and some just get better at hiding it (opens in a new tab).

Picture: a researcher pushes an AI's goal dial and it springs back. Below, an illustrative bar chart. Bad behaviour is high before punishment. After, a paper cover hides that it is still high.
