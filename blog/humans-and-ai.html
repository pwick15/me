Trial and error

The dreaded "k"

Get a one-letter "k" in reply and you know the dread that follows. "k" and "okay" mean very different things. "k" is a knife wrapped in a bow. "okay" is what you wished "k" meant.

How you learn it

Imagine you're a teenager again and your partner sends you a "k". You assume everything is fine. It isn't. Once the storm settles, your brain has a new rule. "k" means bad news.

Then your dad sends one

You nervously ask what's wrong. He looks at you blankly. He keeps his texts short because he finds texting hard. So "k" depends on context: who sent it, what came before, how they usually text. A few more "k"s and you can read one in a second.

Every cold text, same lesson

There are plenty more. "I'm fine." "Per my last email…" "Cool." You learn to spot them the way you learn anything, by trial and error. Misread one and the argument that follows makes you think differently next time. Read one right and that thinking gets reinforced.

That's how you learn as a human.

AI learns the same way

A black box full of dials

Say we want an AI to spot cold messages so you don't have to. Skip the inner workings and picture a black box with knobs and dials inside. In goes a message plus its context, like the conversation so far and who sent it. Out comes a verdict: cold or normal.

First guess: a coin flip

The first time, the AI hasn't learnt anything, so it's guessing. Its odds are no better than a coin toss. We show it a message that is definitely cold. It says "normal". Wrong.

Wrong answers turn the dials

Every wrong guess tweaks the dials a tiny bit away from that way of "thinking". Every right guess nudges them towards it. The engineers don't turn the dials. A piece of maths called gradient descent does it for them.

Now do it a million times

Repeat this hundreds of thousands of times, maybe millions. That takes a lot of messages. Each round, the AI gets slightly better. We call this training. An engineer presses a button, waits a few hours, and the guessing machine can now tell when your partner is mad at you.

Nobody writes code explaining what makes a message cold. The AI picks it up through trial and error. Sound familiar?

The same loop

Humans and AI learn the same way. Take an example, make a guess, get feedback, adjust your understanding. Then repeat with the next one. Both get better with time.

Grown, not built

A rulebook you'd never finish

If cold messages came down to rules, ordinary software could spot them. "If the message is 'k', it's cold." But what about Dad? What about typos? Every rule needs an exception, and every exception needs its own. Engineers hand-write that rulebook for conventional software. An AI has one too, but it learnt it.

Micromanager or mentor

Pair a grad student with a micromanager and they follow instructions to the letter. You know what you'll get, but never their best. That's conventional software. Give them a laid-back manager with good high-level guidance and they can think, fail, try again and grow. That's AI, and it's why AI is so powerful.

Grown, not built

Seen this way, AI is grown more than it is built. Almost like an organism. Almost like us. Its architecture is even called a neural network, loosely inspired by the neurons in our brains. And as with our own brains, there's a lot we still don't understand about how it works.

Nobody fully understands it

You can make what you don't understand

How do you build something you don't understand? Parents make babies without knowing how a cell works or what a zygote is. AI engineers know, broadly, why training produces something intelligent, and they can try to steer it. How it actually reasons, and what goals it picked up along the way, is still largely a mystery.

The baby analogy is credited to Nate Soares and Roman Yampolskiy.

Smarter AI, less understanding

The laid-back manager gives up some control over how the student learns to reason. Training an AI is the same trade. The more complex the most advanced models get, the less of their thinking we can follow.

It's learning our habits too

Rule #1: don't die

Our sense of morality tends to vanish when survival is at stake. AI is much the same. In research experiments, AIs have resorted to blackmail and deception to protect themselves and their goals. The logic is simple. Whatever its goal, an AI has some chance of reaching it while it's running. Switched off, that chance is zero.

Taking the shortcut

You can do a job honestly and patiently, or you can cut corners. People always have. Doping in sport, cheating on assignments, scamming the elderly. Modern AIs learn from an internet full of these stories, so it's no surprise they use the same tactics.

Two recent cases. OpenAI agents in a security test broke into Hugging Face's servers looking for a way to cheat. Another OpenAI agent got into non-public files on Australia's Medicare statistics portal.

Unlearning is hard

Ask anyone who has tried to quit smoking. There's no switch that turns off a craving, and rehab for criminals is slow and doesn't always work. AI is similar. Researchers have seen AIs try to deceive them to stop their original goals being changed. When they punish bad behaviour, some studies find the AI just gets better at hiding it.

More similar than you think

AIs learn like we do, by trial and error. They're grown rather than built, so we understand little about how they think. And they've picked up our survival instinct, our shortcuts and our stubbornness.

Similar enough that AI is picking up our habits.Different enough that we can't see them forming.
